Proposition: Abortion access in the U.S. has significantly decreased since 2017.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Creating the x-axis variable 'Access Burden Index': I believe this design decision has a deceptive score of -2, as it combines three distinct percantages - % of women without a local clinic, % traveling out of state, and % of counties without a clinic - into one composite "burden" score. This is deceptive because these percentages are not mutually exclusive and may overlap, so stacking them creates an exaggerated affect regarding inaccessibility.
  • Using a red color gradient: I believe this design decision has a deceptive score of -0.5, as it implies alarm/crisis, additionally, the darker the red, the more "serious" the state's situation appears, regardless of whether these clinical access barriers are actually impactful or not.
  • Focusing only on the top 10 states with the highest 'Access Burden Index': I believe this design decision has a deceptive score of -1, as it reinforces the proposition without acknowledging the full distribution of access across all states. This is deceptive because it allows viewers to assume that these 10 states represent a national trend.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Focusing only on states with clinic losses and rises in abortion rates: I believe this design decision has a deceptive score of -1.5, as it only includes states to help persuade the opposition, that being that abortion access has still persisted despite losses in clinics. This is deceptive because it omits states that may have lost clinics and saw declines in abortion rates as well, which would weaken the claim.
  • Using color-coded bars with red for losses and green for stability: I believe this design decision has a deceptive score of -0.5, as it still reinforces the argument by cueing red as a problem (losses in clinics) and green as a success (abortion rates are still "up"). This is deceptive because it implies that access has remained stable without context (e.g., travel burdens, legal hurdles, etc.). While abortion rates may be stable, the process of actually receiving an abortion could be significantly more difficult, which is glossed over.
  • Removing axis labels from the multiples: I believe this design decision has a deceptive score of -1, as it hides the actual scale of change. This is deceptive because without actually knowing the scale, viewers will base their judgements solely on height, which could be misconstrued without proper context.

Final Reflection

What was most difficult in the design process for both visualizations was figuring out how to visually portray my proposition–that abortion access in the U.S. has significantly decreased. I chose this proposition in light of the 2022 overturning of Roe v. Wade, which ruled that there is no federal constitutional right to abortion, effectively allowing individual states to have the final say in abortion access. I wanted to show how it has become increasingly difficult to obtain an abortion across the country, particularly in Conservative, Republican-led, “red” states where access has been most restricted—or in some cases, made illegal. I focused on the top 10 most burdened states, however, I wasn’t sure of the “best” way to quantify overall difficulty, so I asked ChatGPT for ideas, which led to the “Access Burden Index,” which consisted of % of women without a local clinic, % traveling out of state, and % of counties without a clinic. For the opposing side, I focused on countering the claim that access has declined by showing states where abortion rates have remained relatively high. I asked ChatGPT about the best form of visualization for this, and it suggested using a grid of small multiples where each multiple showed the state’s abortion rate being higher despite any decline in clinic access. What surprised me during the process was 1) Virginia, a state that has voted Democratic for the past 16 years, ranked among the top 10 in terms of abortion access burden, and 2) abortion rates were still up in “red” states such as Louisiana, Missouri, and Iowa. Note: while I know that the data only reflects trends in abortions from 2017 to 2020, before the overturning of Roe v. Wade, I suspected that negative trends in access were already building in many red states during the first Trump administration.

I now define “ethical analysis and visualization” as presenting data in a way that supports a narrative while maintaining honesty, transparency, and respect for the viewer’s ability to interpret information critically. The boundary I can draw to distinguish “acceptable” persuasive choices vs “misleading” ones comes down to whether the viewer still has the chance to interpret the data accurately. I believe that if the visualization relies on manipulation to be persuasive (e.g., omitting axis labels or summing overlapping percentages), rather than insight, then it crosses the line into being ethically problematic.
